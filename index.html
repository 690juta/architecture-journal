02A — THE BUILDING, DISASSEMBLED

Building anatomy

A building is an assembly of connected systems. Each part has a specific job, and the performance of the whole depends on how those parts meet.

HOW TO READ THE BUILDING

Think in systems, not isolated objects.

Start at the ground and work upwards. Ask three questions about every component: what does it support, what does it protect, and what other component must it connect to?

The diagram is conceptual, not a construction detail. Actual buildings vary according to their structural system, site, climate, materials, and engineering design.

01

BELOW GROUND

Substructure

The parts of a building that transfer its loads into the ground.

  • Soil / bearing ground — the natural or improved ground that receives the building load.
  • Foundation — spreads or transfers loads to soil or rock. Common types include pads, strips, rafts, and piles.
  • Ground beams — connect foundations or support walls and floors, depending on the design.
  • Damp-proofing and waterproofing — help limit moisture entering the structure.

Key question: is the ground capable of safely supporting the building without excessive settlement?

02

LOAD-BEARING FRAME

Primary structure

The skeleton that keeps the building stable and carries its main loads.

  • Columns — vertical members that carry loads downwards.
  • Beams — horizontal members that collect loads from floors or roofs and transfer them to supports.
  • Slabs — floor or roof plates that carry people, furniture, partitions, and other loads.
  • Load-bearing walls — walls designed to carry structural loads, not merely divide rooms.
  • Bracing / shear walls — resist sideways forces such as wind and earthquake action.
  • Connections — joints that transfer forces between structural members.

Key question: where does the load travel, and what prevents the building from swaying or collapsing?

03

WEATHER BARRIER

Building envelope

The outer boundary separating indoor conditions from the outdoor environment.

  • External walls / façade — protect interior spaces and contribute to thermal, acoustic, and weather performance.
  • Roof assembly — covering, underlay or membrane, structure, insulation, and ventilation where required.
  • Windows and doors — provide openings for daylight, views, access, and ventilation while controlling weather and security.
  • Flashing and sealants — manage water at joints, edges, and openings.
  • Insulation and air / vapour control layers — help manage heat, air leakage, and moisture movement when correctly designed.

Key question: how does the envelope control rain, sun, heat, wind, noise, and unwanted air or moisture?

04

SPACE AND ACCESS

Internal components

The elements that organise rooms, circulation, privacy, and everyday use.

  • Partitions — divide interior spaces; most are non-load-bearing, but some walls are structural.
  • Internal doors — connect rooms and control privacy, access, and movement.
  • Stairs and ramps — connect different floor levels.
  • Ceilings — conceal services, improve appearance, and may contribute to acoustic or fire performance.
  • Built-in elements — cabinetry, counters, and fixed storage support the use of a space.

Key question: can people move through the building safely, comfortably, and intuitively?

05

BUILDING SERVICES

Mechanical, electrical and plumbing

The networks that make a building functional, healthy, and comfortable.

  • Electrical — incoming power, distribution boards, wiring, lighting, outlets, and earthing.
  • Water supply — pipes, pumps, tanks, valves, and fixtures that deliver clean water.
  • Sanitary plumbing — wastewater and soil pipes that carry used water away.
  • HVAC — heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning equipment and distribution.
  • Fire and life safety — alarms, detection, emergency lighting, suppression, and escape provisions.
  • Data and communications — networks, internet, security, and building controls.

Key question: where do the services run, and how can they be inspected and maintained?

06

VISIBLE SURFACES

Finishes and fixtures

The layers people see and touch, applied over the underlying construction.

  • Floor finishes — tile, timber, stone, vinyl, terrazzo, or polished concrete.
  • Wall finishes — paint, plaster, cladding, tiles, panels, or exposed masonry.
  • Ceiling finishes — paint, plasterboard, timber, metal, or acoustic panels.
  • Fixtures and fittings — taps, sanitaryware, handles, lights, switches, and hardware.

Key question: is the finish suitable for its use, exposure, cleaning needs, and expected wear?

07

OUTSIDE THE FOOTPRINT

Site works and drainage

The landscape and infrastructure that allow the building to function on its site.

  • Site grading — shapes ground levels to guide surface water away from the building.
  • Surface drainage — gutters, downpipes, channels, and drains collect and direct rainwater.
  • External paving — paths, driveways, terraces, and access routes.
  • Retaining structures — hold back soil where ground levels change.
  • Landscape — planting and shaded areas influence comfort, ecology, and site water behaviour.

Key question: where does rainwater go, and does the site remain accessible and stable?

08

PUTTING IT TOGETHER

How the systems work together

A simplified way to understand the relationship between the major building systems.

Roof and floor loads↓Beams / load-bearing walls↓Columns / structural walls↓Foundations↓Soil or rock

At the same time, the envelope keeps weather out, services bring in and remove essential resources, and internal components make the space usable. Finishes complete the surfaces but do not replace the structure beneath them.

A NOTE FOR TROPICAL BUILDINGS

Moisture management is fundamental.

In Indonesia’s hot, humid and often rainy climate, pay particular attention to roof overhangs, waterproofing, drainage falls, ventilation, shaded openings, corrosion protection, mould-resistant detailing, and materials suited to repeated wetting. A beautiful finish cannot compensate for a leaking roof or poorly drained site.

02B — THE STRUCTURAL SKELETON

Structural systems

Study the essential structural elements through monochrome, isometric 3D models. Each model can be rotated with the pointer or touch to inspect its form.

Structure is the load-resisting system of a building. It must safely carry gravity loads—such as the building’s own weight, occupants, and furniture—and actions such as wind and earthquakes. The examples below are conceptual teaching models, not engineering details or construction specifications.

01 / SUBSTRUCTURE

Foundation

A foundation transfers the building’s loads to soil or rock. A spread footing enlarges the contact area so the ground pressure is reduced.

Common forms
Pad, strip, raft, and pile foundations.
Design concern
Bearing capacity, settlement, groundwater, and soil conditions.
VERTICAL MEMBER

02 / VERTICAL SUPPORT

Column

A column is a vertical structural member. It receives forces from beams or slabs and transfers them down to foundations.

Typical materials
Reinforced concrete, structural steel, or timber.
Design concern
Compression, buckling, slenderness, and connection detailing.
HORIZONTAL MEMBER

03 / SPAN AND TRANSFER

Beam

A beam spans between supports. It carries loads from floors, roofs, or walls and transfers the resulting forces to columns or structural walls.

Typical materials
Reinforced concrete, steel, or engineered timber.
Design concern
Bending, shear, deflection, and support connections.
HORIZONTAL PLATE

04 / FLOOR AND ROOF PLATE

Slab

A slab forms a floor or roof surface. It supports people, furniture, partitions, and other loads, then transfers them to beams, walls, or columns.

Common systems
One-way, two-way, flat plate, ribbed, and composite slabs.
Design concern
Span, thickness, reinforcement, vibration, and deflection.
TRIANGULATED FRAME

05 / LONG-SPAN ROOF

Truss

A truss is an assembly of connected members arranged into triangles. Its geometry allows it to span larger distances efficiently.

Common forms
King-post, queen-post, Fink, Pratt, and Warren trusses.
Design concern
Member forces, joints, lateral restraint, and roof bracing.
LOAD-BEARING WALL

06 / CONTINUOUS SUPPORT

Load-bearing wall

A load-bearing wall supports loads from floors or roofs above and transfers them to the foundation. It is different from a partition designed only to divide rooms.

Common systems
Brick or block masonry, reinforced concrete, and structural timber walls.
Design concern
Openings, wall stability, vertical loads, and lateral resistance.
LATERAL STABILITY

07 / LATERAL RESISTANCE

Bracing and shear walls

Bracing and shear walls resist horizontal actions such as wind and earthquake forces. They help limit sideways movement and keep the structural frame stable.

Common systems
Diagonal steel bracing, reinforced concrete shear walls, and moment frames.
Design concern
Continuity, stiffness, torsion, and a clear load path to the foundations.

THE MOST IMPORTANT IDEA

Follow the load.

Loads should travel through a continuous, intentional structural path. A break or weak connection in that path can compromise the whole system.

ROOF / FLOOR↓SLAB↓BEAM OR WALL↓COLUMN↓FOUNDATION↓GROUND

03 — WHAT BUILDINGS ARE MADE OF

Materials library

Compare materials by appearance, performance, installation, maintenance, and suitability—not appearance alone.

FLOORING / 01

Porcelain tile

Dense ceramic tile with low water absorption. Often used in living areas, kitchens, bathrooms, and high-traffic spaces.

Consider: slip resistance, surface finish, grout, and installation.

FLOORING / 02

Hardwood

Natural timber flooring available as solid boards or engineered products with a real-wood wear layer.

Consider: humidity, movement, maintenance, and refinishing.

FLOORING / 03

Terrazzo

Decorative aggregate embedded in a cementitious or resin binder; available poured in place or as tiles.

Consider: binder type, sealing, joints, and surface finish.

FLOORING / 04

Concrete

Can be polished, stained, exposed, or coated to create a durable contemporary surface.

Consider: cracking, joints, moisture, and sealers.

FLOORING / 05

Vinyl / SPC

Synthetic flooring in plank or tile formats. SPC uses a rigid mineral-filled core.

Consider: wear layer, subfloor flatness, joints, and heat.

FLOORING / 06

Natural stone

Marble, granite, travertine, slate, and limestone offer distinct natural patterns and properties.

Consider: porosity, sealing, hardness, and slip resistance.

WALLS / 07

Brick masonry

Traditional masonry used for walls and sometimes exposed as a finish.

Consider: structural role, mortar, moisture, and thermal performance.

FINISHES / 08

Gypsum board

Board system commonly used for interior partitions and suspended ceilings.

Consider: framing, moisture-rated products, fire ratings, and detailing.

ENVELOPE / 09

Glass

Used in windows, doors, partitions, and façades to transmit light and connect spaces.

Consider: safety glazing, solar gain, privacy, and thermal performance.

STRUCTURE / 10

Reinforced concrete

Concrete combined with steel reinforcement to resist compression and tension.

Consider: structural design, durability, cover, and exposure conditions.

STRUCTURE / 11

Structural steel

Steel sections used in frames, long-span structures, roof systems, and connections.

Consider: corrosion protection, fire protection, and connection design.

STRUCTURE / 12

Timber

Used in roof structures, floors, doors, façades, and furniture.

Consider: species, moisture, treatment, grading, and connections.

Material performance varies by product and installation. Always consult technical data sheets and local requirements before specifying a material.
